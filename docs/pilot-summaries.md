## HB1772

Medium · 386 source words · 154 summary words · [raw response](../data/pilot/responses/HB1772.json)

The bill would limit negligence claims against higher education institutions for admitting or enrolling a person with a prior criminal conviction, offering that person housing, or otherwise helping the person pursue education. An institution could not be held liable solely because of the prior conviction, and evidence of the conviction would not be allowed in such a claim.

These limits would not apply if the institution knew the person had a prior conviction for a violent or violent sexual offense. They also would not apply if the institution knew the person committed such an offense after admission and willfully allowed the person to keep enrolling, living there, or using the institution. The bill would not create or expand a right to sue. It defines higher education institutions broadly to include entities offering postsecondary education, credentials, or training. It would take effect July 1, 2026, and apply to claims arising on or after that date.

## HB0800

Long Main · 963 source words · 273 summary words · [raw response](../data/pilot/responses/HB0800.json)

The bill would prohibit public schools, public charter schools, and public colleges from discriminating against students or employees on listed grounds, including race, ethnicity, national origin, disability, religion, and marital status. It also lists sex in several protections and gender in its ban on exclusion from federally or state-assisted public education programs and employment practices. Admission criteria could not restrict access based on the listed grounds. Classes, services, programs, and opportunities would have to be available without discrimination, while programs for students with particular needs or talents could continue.

These institutions would have to treat antisemitic harassment or discrimination the same way they treat discrimination motivated by race. They would have to add the International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance’s May 26, 2016, definition of antisemitism and related prohibited conduct to their codes of conduct. The bill encourages, but does not require, antisemitism training and Jewish American history curricula.

The state education department would designate a Title VI coordinator for K–12 schools, and each public college governing board would designate one for its institution. Institutions would report antisemitism incidents and complaints to their coordinator, who would investigate complaints. If an investigation found that an institution had engaged in, allowed, or insufficiently prohibited antisemitic discrimination, it would receive written notice to address the concerns within 30 days. If it did not take the necessary action, the responsible agency would file a federal Title VI complaint. Coordinators would report annually to the General Assembly by June 30.

The bill says comparable criticism of Israel is not antisemitic, preserves constitutional rights, and must not conflict with state or federal discrimination laws. It would take effect upon becoming law.

## HB1889

Long Main · 1,118 source words · 379 summary words · [raw response](../data/pilot/responses/HB1889.json)

The bill would add income, testing, audit, and reporting rules to Tennessee’s education freedom scholarship program, beginning July 1, 2026. To qualify under a new provision, a student would have to live in a household whose income for the previous year did not exceed 300% of the amount required to qualify for free or reduced-price lunch under federal guidelines. Scholarship applicants would have to name the school they attended the previous year. The bill would also delete two existing subdivisions of the eligibility statute without showing their contents.

Scholarship recipients would have to take the same Tennessee comprehensive assessment program tests, or authorized successor tests, given to public school students in the same grade or course. A private school would have to administer a test uniformly to recipients in the same grade, except when a recipient has an accommodation under the cited disability laws. Schools enrolling recipients would have to give parents or guardians written progress reports at least twice a year, limit a recipient’s tuition increase to 10% over the prior year, publish specified admissions and school information and recipients’ test outcomes, and voluntarily comply with the cited open meetings and open records laws.

The comptroller could audit the program and private schools enrolling recipients. The education department and audited schools would have to cooperate; a school would pay for its audit. Schools would also have to obtain an annual audit from a certified public accountant covering scholarship funds received, expenditures over $25,000, and the names, titles, and salaries of their five highest-paid employees. They would submit those audits to the department by August 1. By October 1 each year, the department would publish and send the legislature a report covering participating schools, recipient counts and test outcomes, applicants’ counties of residence, prior-year scholarship spending, known violations, and school audit reports. The comptroller would report on recipients’ educational outcomes, program administration costs, and any effect on local education agencies by December 31, 2029. The bill would repeal this part of the law on July 1, 2030.

The bill would also delete subsection 49-6-3508(b) and add references to scholarship recipients, the program, and private schools enrolling recipients in two provisions of section 49-6-5101. The supplied text does not show the provisions being deleted or the surrounding language being amended.

## HB1732

Long Tail · 2,135 source words · 263 summary words · [raw response](../data/pilot/responses/HB1732.json)

The bill would add a nonbinding guide to Tennessee law listing training requirements and opportunities for educators, school personnel, and volunteers at local education agencies (LEAs) and public charter schools. It groups the listed training by whether the cited law calls for it annually, every three years, before certain activities, or as an opportunity. Topics include school safety, student health, child abuse, bullying, and transportation. The list would not be exhaustive and would not excuse anyone from other training requirements. The bill would also add references to the guide in the cited training provisions.

The bill would require LEAs and public charter schools to ensure that teachers, administrators, and other school personnel receive de-escalation training within their first year of employment and every three years afterward. They would have to ensure that officials and employees working directly with students complete child abuse training on the same schedule. For that training, employees could take a program identified by the education department or one meeting guidelines set by the department of children's services. Each LEA and charter school would have to report its compliance to the education department.

At the beginning of each school year, LEAs would have to give teachers and school counselors a copy of their bullying and harassment policy and information about its implementation and prevention strategies. LEAs would also have to train teachers and counselors on the policy within their first year of employment and every three years afterward. The education department would have to provide training guidelines and recommend free prevention resources. The bill would take effect July 1, 2026.
